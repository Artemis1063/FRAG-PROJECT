# Perfumery principles for the Layering Hub

The rules the Layer tab scores with, each tied to where it comes from. Researched
2026-10-28. Most published layering advice is retailer or blog content, so each
rule is marked by how well it's established:

- **Established**: standard perfumery reference material.
- **Consensus**: repeated across many independent layering guides.
- **Judgement**: a reasonable rule of thumb with weaker sourcing; used with a
  smaller effect.

## 1. Notes evaporate in three stages (Established)

Perfume materials are classed by volatility (Poucher's evaporation
coefficients, 1–100): **top notes** are small, light molecules that are smelled
first and fade fastest; **heart notes** form the body; **base notes** are the
least volatile, give depth and persistence, and often act as fixatives for the
lighter notes.

**Used for:** base and heart notes carry more weight than top notes in "How it
smells" (base 40%, heart 35%, top 25%, as in dupe matching), and the base decides
which perfume is "heavier".

## 2. The fragrance wheel: neighbours blend, opposites contrast (Established)

Michael Edwards' fragrance wheel arranges families in a circle so that
neighbouring families share traits and blend most easily, and families farther
apart contrast. Its current form has four groups (Fresh, Floral, Amber, Woody)
and 14 families, in this circular order:

Fruity → Floral → Soft Floral → Floral Amber → Soft Amber → Amber → Woody Amber
→ Woods → Mossy Woods → Dry Woods → Aromatic (Fougère) → Citrus → Water → Green
→ (back to Fruity)

**Used for:** each note family in our data sits on a wheel position, and two
perfumes' families earn affinity by wheel distance:

| Our note family | Wheel position |
|---|---|
| Fruit | Fruity |
| Floral, White floral | Floral |
| Powdery | Soft Floral |
| Spice | Floral Amber |
| Gourmand, Resin & incense | Soft Amber |
| Amber | Amber |
| Oud | Woody Amber |
| Wood | Woods |
| Earth & moss | Mossy Woods |
| Smoke & leather | Dry Woods |
| Aromatic herb | Aromatic (Fougère) |
| Citrus | Citrus |
| Marine & mineral | Water |
| Green | Green |
| Musk | none (see rule 4) |

| Wheel distance | Affinity |
|---|---|
| 0 (same family) | +1.5 harmonious, little contrast |
| 1 (neighbours) | +2 blend best |
| 2 | +1 |
| 3 | 0 |
| 4–5 | −0.5 |
| 6–7 (opposite) | −1 strong contrast |

## 3. Classic pairings (Consensus)

Pairings recommended again and again in layering guides override the wheel
with +2: amber + musk; vanilla (gourmand) + woods such as cedar; rose
(floral) + sandalwood or cedar (woods); citrus + green florals; and
perfumes that share a dominant note such as vanilla, sandalwood or bergamot
(see rule 6).

## 4. Musk blends with everything (Established, as a fixative)

Musks are base-note fixatives used across every family, so musk never causes a
clash: affinity +1 with every family, and it doesn't count toward "heavy"
(rule 7).

## 5. Known clashes (Consensus)

These override the wheel with −2:
- **Heavy amber/oriental with fresh aquatic** (Amber, Soft Amber, Woody Amber or
  Oud with Marine & mineral): profiles too far apart to harmonise.
- **Sugary gourmand with sharp green** (Gourmand with Green): reported to give a
  chemical off-note.

## 6. Shared notes act as a bridge (Consensus)

Two perfumes that share at least one dominant note hang together better. Shared
notes add a bonus, and rare shared notes count more than common ones (the same
rarity weighting as dupe matching).

## 7. Don't stack two heavy bases (Consensus)

Two heavy perfumes (both with bases dominated by amber, oud, resin & incense or
gourmand) combine into something overwhelming. Penalty when both are heavy;
the advice suggests pairing a heavy base with a lighter, brighter partner.

## 8. Two loud citrus openings collide (Judgement)

Two perfumes that both open on strong citrus peak at the same time and can clash
before either develops. Small penalty, and the advice staggers them (spray one,
wait, then the other).

## 9. Classic accords complete each other (Established)

Some combinations are named structures in perfumery. When the two perfumes
together supply the parts of one, "How it smells" gets a bonus and the reason
names it:
- **Chypre:** bergamot (citrus top) + labdanum/rose/jasmine (heart) + oakmoss base.
- **Fougère:** lavender + coumarin/tonka + oakmoss.
- **Amber:** labdanum + vanilla + benzoin.

## 10. How to apply a layered pair (Consensus)

- **Heavier first, lighter on top.** Heaviness is about the base (woods, amber,
  oud, vanilla, musk vs bright citrus tops). Give the first one a minute or two
  to settle before the second.
- **Same pulse point** (direct layering) for a unified blend when the pair
  harmonises.
- **Different pulse points** (spatial layering) for contrast or a riskier pair:
  the heavier one on lower pulse points (wrists, inner elbows), the lighter one
  higher (neck, behind the ears), so the scent shifts as people come closer.
- **Fewer sprays,** building up gradually; when one is much louder, use less of it.
- **Test on skin,** since skin chemistry changes the result: blotter first, then
  skin, then wait about 15 minutes. A sour, metallic or chemical edge within
  minutes is a sign of a clash.

## What these rules can't know

Real perfumers judge by smelling, and skin chemistry varies. The page says the
scores are a guide based on published notes and these principles, and suggests
testing on skin.
